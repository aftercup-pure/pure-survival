Entry 00

Priorities — The Rule of Threes

When everything feels urgent, this ordering tells you what actually kills fastest. Work top to bottom; don't skip ahead to what feels comfortable.

3 minutes without air, or in icy water — protect breathing and stop severe bleeding first.
3 hours without shelter in harsh conditions (exposure, storm) — core temperature kills faster than thirst or hunger.
3 days without water — dehydration impairs judgment long before this point.
3 weeks without food — uncomfortable and weakening, but rarely the immediate threat.

First 10 minutes

  1. Stop. Don't move on instinct. Sit down if you can.
  2. Check yourself and others for injury — control bleeding before anything else (see Entry 06, First Aid).
  3. Assess immediate threats: fire, water, unstable ground, weather closing in, animals.
  4. Decide: stay put or move? (see Entry 13, Mindset) — most rescues find people who stayed near their last known point.
  5. Take stock of what you're carrying before you start building or searching for anything.
Note

This manual covers general wilderness and emergency preparedness knowledge. It is not a substitute for certified wilderness first aid, CPR, or survival training — take a real course before you need one.

Entry 01

Water

Step by step
  1. If it's cloudy or has debris, filter it through cloth first, then let it settle.
  2. Bring it to a rolling boil for at least 1 minute (3 minutes above ~2000m) — the single most reliable method.
  3. No fire available? Use purification tablets and wait the full contact time on the label.
  4. Let it cool, then store it in a clean, covered container.

Finding it

  • Follow terrain downhill — water moves down. Green, low-lying vegetation often marks it.
  • Look for animal tracks converging, insect swarms (especially at dawn/dusk), and birds circling or flying low in a consistent direction morning and evening.
  • Dig at the base of concave river bends or in dry stream beds — water can be just below the surface.
  • Collect dew with cloth dragged over grass at dawn, or rainwater in any wide container, tarp, or dug-and-lined pit.
  • A solar still (plastic sheet over a pit with vegetation, weighted in the center over a collection cup) yields small amounts but works with zero fuel.

Making it safe

MethodHowKills
BoilingRolling boil 1 minute (3 min above ~2000m)Bacteria, viruses, parasites
Purification tabletsIodine or chlorine dioxide, per package instructions, wait full contact timeMost pathogens (check label for cysts)
FiltrationCommercial filter rated ≤0.2 micron, or cloth + sand/charcoal/gravel layers as pre-filter onlySediment, some parasites — not viruses
UVSteripen-type device, clear water onlyBacteria, viruses, parasites
Tip

Improvised filtration (cloth, sand, charcoal) removes particles but not pathogens — always boil or chemically treat filtered water before drinking it.

Rationing and signs of trouble

  • Don't ration water once you have it — drink what you need; ration your sweat instead by resting in shade and moving in cooler hours.
  • Dark urine, headache, dizziness, and dry mouth are early dehydration signs — treat them immediately, don't wait for thirst.
  • Never drink seawater or urine — both accelerate dehydration.
Entry 02

Fire

Step by step
  1. Prepare a fist-sized nest of tinder before you strike anything.
  2. Sort kindling by size — pencil-thin first, finger-thick next — within easy reach.
  3. Ignite the tinder nest, then cup or shield it from wind as it catches.
  4. Once it flames, feed in the smallest kindling first, then build up gradually.
  5. Only add fuel-sized wood once the fire is burning steadily on its own.

The three ingredients

  • Tinder — catches a spark: dry grass, birch bark, cotton fluff, fine wood shavings, dryer lint.
  • Kindling — pencil-thin to finger-thick dry twigs that carry the flame up.
  • Fuel — wrist-thick and larger dry wood, added gradually once the fire is established.

Ignition methods

  1. Lighter / matches — always carry redundant, waterproofed sources.
  2. Ferro rod (flint & steel) — scrape a shower of sparks directly into fine tinder; works wet, works cold.
  3. Lens — magnifying glass, eyeglasses, or a clear water-filled bottle/bag can focus sunlight onto tinder on a bright day.
  4. Friction (bow drill) — reliable but skill-dependent; needs a soft, dry spindle/board wood pairing (cedar, willow, cottonwood) and real practice beforehand.
  5. Battery + steel wool — touch both terminals to fine steel wool to ignite it instantly.

Fire lays

  • Teepee — fast-burning, good for quick heat/light and getting a fire established.
  • Log cabin — stacked square, burns steady and long, good for cooking coals.
  • Lean-to — kindling leaned against a support log over tinder, shelters the flame from wind.
Warning

Never start a fire inside an unventilated shelter, tent, snow cave, or vehicle — carbon monoxide kills quietly and quickly. Clear a wide radius down to bare earth in dry conditions before lighting anything.

Entry 03

Shelter

Step by step — debris hut
  1. Pick a site: high enough to stay dry, sheltered from wind, no dead branches overhead.
  2. Lean a ridge pole against a tree fork or support, about hip-height.
  3. Rib both sides with sticks angled down from the ridge to the ground.
  4. Pile leaves, brush, or debris over the ribs at least 0.5m thick.
  5. Line the inside floor with a dry insulating layer before you get in.

Site selection

  • High enough to avoid flooding and cold air pooling, but sheltered from wind — not a ridge top, not a valley floor.
  • No dead branches overhead ("widowmakers"), no dry stream beds (flash flood risk), not directly under a lone tall tree in storm season.
  • Close to water and firewood if it's safe to be, without camping right at the water's edge.

Types

  • Debris hut — a ridge pole against a support, ribbed with sticks, piled thick (0.5m+) with leaves/brush for insulation. Best all-around cold-weather shelter with zero tools.
  • Lean-to — angled roof against a horizontal support, open on one side, faced away from wind; fast to build, pair with a reflective fire wall.
  • Snow cave / quinzhee — mound and hollow out snow, or dig into a deep drift; surprisingly warm (can hold near 0°C when it's far colder outside) — always poke a ventilation hole.
  • Tarp/poncho shelter — fastest option if you're carrying material; A-frame or lean-to configuration.

Insulation rule

Ground insulation matters as much as overhead cover — a thick layer of dry leaves, pine boughs, or grass between you and bare ground prevents conductive heat loss, which is often faster than losing heat to the air.

Entry 04

Food & Foraging

Food is rarely the immediate emergency — don't take foraging risks in the first 72 hours unless you're certain of an identification.

Universal edibility test (last resort, one plant part at a time)

  1. Separate the plant into parts (root, leaf, stem, flower) — test only one part at a time.
  2. Smell it; discard anything with an almond or bitter odor.
  3. Rub a piece on your inner wrist, wait 15 minutes for a reaction.
  4. If no reaction, hold a small piece to your lips for 3 minutes, then to your tongue without swallowing for 15 minutes.
  5. If still no burning or numbness, chew and swallow a small amount, then wait 8 hours without eating anything else.
  6. If no ill effect, the part is likely safe in modest quantity — repeat for each plant part separately.
Warning

Never eat mushrooms using this test — many toxic species show no symptoms until organ damage is already underway. Only eat mushrooms you can identify with certainty.

Easier calories first

  • Insects (grubs, crickets, ants) are safer and more available than most foraged plants — avoid anything brightly colored or foul-smelling.
  • Simple fishing: a hand line with any hook-shaped piece of metal, wire, or a carved gorge hook baited with insects.
  • Passive trapping (snares on game trails) is far more efficient than active hunting for a solo, weakened person — check lines rather than waiting at them.
Entry 05

Navigation

Without a compass

  • Shadow-stick method: place a stick upright, mark the tip of its shadow, wait 15 minutes, mark the new tip — the line between marks runs roughly east–west (first mark = west).
  • Analog watch: point the hour hand at the sun; the line halfway between the hour hand and 12 points roughly south (northern hemisphere) or north (southern hemisphere).
  • Stars: in the northern hemisphere, locate the Big Dipper and follow the two outer bowl stars to Polaris (the North Star) — it sits within about 1° of true north and doesn't move.
  • Moss is an unreliable indicator — it grows wherever it's damp, not reliably on one side of trees. Don't rely on it.
Step by step — shadow-stick
  1. Push a straight stick about 1m long into open, flat ground.
  2. Mark the tip of its shadow with a small stone — this is roughly west.
  3. Wait 15–20 minutes without moving the stick.
  4. Mark the new shadow tip — this is roughly east.
  5. Draw a line between the two marks: that line runs west to east.

Improvised compass

Magnetize a steel needle by stroking it 40–50 times in one direction against silk, wool, or your hair. Float it on a small leaf in still water — it will align north–south.

Staying oriented

  • Before moving, pick a landmark and note your direction of travel; look back occasionally so the return route is recognizable.
  • Follow a stream downhill if you're trying to reach civilization — it usually leads to larger water and eventually settlement.
  • Mark your path (broken branches, stacked stones/cairns) so you can retrace it and so searchers can follow it.

For marked trails in Hungary specifically, see Entry 14, Trail Map.

Entry 06

First Aid

Warning

This section is a memory-jog, not training. Take an accredited first aid / CPR course. In any populated area, call local emergency services before or while treating a serious injury.

Severe bleeding

  1. Apply firm, direct pressure with the cleanest cloth available. Don't remove it to check — add more on top if it soaks through.
  2. Elevate the injury above heart level if possible.
  3. If bleeding is life-threatening and pressure alone isn't controlling it, apply a tourniquet 5–7cm above the wound (not on a joint), tighten until bleeding stops, and note the time applied.

Choking (conscious adult)

  1. Ask "are you choking?" — if they can't speak or cough, act.
  2. 5 firm back blows between the shoulder blades, then 5 abdominal thrusts (Heimlich maneuver): fist above the navel, grasp with other hand, pull sharply inward and upward.
  3. Alternate 5-and-5 until the object clears or the person loses consciousness — then begin CPR.

Unconscious but breathing (recovery position)

Step by step
  1. Kneel beside the person; place the arm nearest you out at a right angle to their body.
  2. Bring their far arm across their chest, back of the hand against their near cheek.
  3. Grab the far knee, pull it up, and roll them toward you onto their side.
  4. Tilt the head back slightly to keep the airway open; recheck breathing regularly.
  5. Call for emergency help and stay with them until it arrives.

CPR (adult, rough guide)

  1. Check responsiveness and breathing. If absent, start compressions immediately.
  2. Hands centered on the chest, compress hard and fast — about 5–6cm deep, 100–120 compressions per minute (roughly the tempo of a fast heartbeat).
  3. 30 compressions to 2 rescue breaths if trained in breaths; compression-only CPR is still effective and simpler for an untrained rescuer.
  4. Continue until help arrives, an AED is available, or the person recovers.

Fractures & sprains

  • Immobilize the joint above and below the injury with a rigid splint (stick, trekking pole, rolled pack) bound firmly but not so tight it cuts circulation.
  • Don't try to straighten an obviously deformed fracture — splint it as found.
  • For sprains: rest, ice/cold water if available, compression wrap, elevate.

Burns

  • Cool with running or clean standing water for 10–20 minutes — don't use ice directly on skin.
  • Don't pop blisters or apply butter, oil, or toothpaste.
  • Cover loosely with a clean, non-fluffy cloth.

Hypothermia

  • Signs: uncontrollable shivering that then stops, confusion, slurred speech, clumsiness ("the umbles" — mumbles, fumbles, stumbles).
  • Get the person dry, insulated from the ground, and out of wind immediately.
  • Warm the core first (torso, neck, head) — skin-to-skin contact in a sleeping bag if severe. Avoid rubbing limbs or giving alcohol.
  • Warm sweet drinks only if the person is fully alert and able to swallow safely.

Heat exhaustion / heatstroke

  • Heat exhaustion: heavy sweating, weakness, nausea — move to shade, cool the body, hydrate with small frequent sips.
  • Heatstroke (medical emergency): hot/dry or flushed skin, confusion, collapse — cool aggressively (wet cloth, fan, immerse limbs in cool water) and get emergency help immediately.
Entry 07

Signaling & Rescue

Step by step
  1. Decide stay-or-go (see Entry 00) — most rescues start at the last known point.
  2. Set up ground-to-air signals now, before you're exhausted: an X or V, or three fires in a triangle.
  3. Keep a signal fire laid and ready to light the instant you hear or see a search party.
  4. Save your whistle, mirror, and any battery power for when a rescuer is actually in range.
  5. Stay visible: bright clothing or a space blanket laid out flat in the open.

Ground-to-air signals

  • The international distress signal is three of anything: three fires in a triangle, three whistle blasts, three flashes, three piles of rocks — repeat, pause, repeat.
  • Stamp or lay out a large "X" (need assistance) or "V" (need help urgently) in an open area, using logs, rocks, or trampled snow — make it as large and high-contrast as possible.
  • Smoke by day (add green boughs to a fire for thick white smoke), bright flame by night.

Tools

  • A whistle carries much farther than a shouting voice and costs no energy — three blasts, pause, repeat.
  • A signal mirror can be seen for miles in daylight — aim by sighting the target through the mirror's aim hole, or flash it broadly across the horizon if unsure.
  • Bright clothing or a space blanket laid flat and weighted down is highly visible from the air.

Stay or go?

If anyone knows your intended route and return time, staying near your last known point is usually the safer bet — most search efforts start there. Move only for a clear reason: no water, direct danger, or a known safe destination within reach.

Entry 08

Reading the Weather

Step by step — storm closing in
  1. Note the warning signs: darkening anvil clouds, sudden calm, dropping temperature.
  2. Get off ridgelines, out of open fields, and away from lone tall trees now, not later.
  3. Head for low, sheltered ground — but not a dry stream bed or gully (flash flood risk).
  4. If lightning is close (under 30 seconds between flash and thunder), crouch low, feet together.
  5. Wait it out; don't move again until roughly 30 minutes after the last thunder.
  • Tall, anvil-shaped clouds (cumulonimbus) — thunderstorm building; seek low ground away from lone trees, ridges, and water.
  • Thin, wispy high clouds (cirrus) thickening and lowering over hours — a front is approaching, expect rain within a day.
  • Sudden still air and dropping temperature before a storm — a warning to secure shelter now.
  • Halo around the sun or moon — often precedes rain or snow within 24 hours.
  • Red sky at sunset — often (not always) fair weather following; red sky at sunrise — often unsettled weather coming.
  • Count seconds between lightning flash and thunder, divide by 3 for distance in km (by 5 for miles) — under 30 seconds (about 10km / 6 miles), take shelter immediately.
Tip

During lightning, avoid open fields, ridgelines, lone tall trees, and water. Crouch low on the balls of your feet if caught in the open, feet together, minimizing ground contact.

Entry 10

Wildlife Safety

General principle

Almost all animal encounters end without incident if the animal has an escape route and doesn't feel cornered or surprised. Make noise while moving through brush, store food away from your sleeping area, and give animals space and a way out.

Step by step — a large animal won't leave
  1. Stop moving toward it. Don't run.
  2. Make yourself look bigger — raise your arms, group up if others are with you.
  3. Speak in a firm, low voice; back away slowly at an angle, never turning your back.
  4. Give it a clear escape route — don't corner it.
  5. Only fight back if actually attacked, targeting the eyes and nose.

Large predators

  • Don't run — it can trigger a chase response. Back away slowly, speak firmly, make yourself look large.
  • Species-specific advice differs (e.g. some bear species: play dead if attacked defensively; others: fight back) — learn the specific animals of the region you're in before you go.

Snakes

  • Most bites happen when a snake is stepped on or handled — watch foot placement, don't reach into holes or under rocks blindly.
  • If bitten: stay calm, keep the bitten limb still and below heart level, remove tight jewelry/clothing before swelling starts, get to medical care — don't cut, suck, ice, or apply a tourniquet.

Insects

  • Ticks: check your whole body daily in tick country; remove with fine tweezers close to the skin, pulling straight out steadily.
  • Mosquitoes: cover up at dawn/dusk in disease-prone regions; repellent and netting are worth the pack weight.
Entry 11

Disaster-Specific Response

Earthquake

Step by step
  1. Drop to the ground immediately.
  2. Take cover under sturdy furniture, away from windows and tall unsecured objects.
  3. Hold on to it until the shaking fully stops.
  4. Outdoors instead: move to open ground away from buildings, trees, and power lines.
  5. After shaking stops: expect aftershocks, check for gas leaks and structural damage before re-entering buildings.

Flood

  • Move to higher ground immediately — don't wait to gather belongings.
  • Never walk or drive through moving water — 15cm can knock an adult down, 30cm can float a vehicle.
  • Avoid contact with floodwater where possible — it's frequently contaminated.

Severe storm / tornado

  • Go to the lowest interior room, away from windows — a basement or interior bathroom/closet.
  • No sturdy building: lie flat in a low ditch or depression, protect your head and neck.

Extended power outage

  • Keep refrigerator/freezer closed — a full freezer holds safe temperature roughly 48 hours unopened.
  • Never run a generator, grill, or camp stove indoors or in a garage — carbon monoxide is odorless and deadly.
  • Keep one phone unplugged and reserved for emergencies to conserve any remaining battery/backup power.
Entry 12

Bug-out Bag Checklist

Water & food

  • Water — at least 2L per person, plus purification tablets/filter
  • High-calorie, no-cook food for 3 days

Warmth & shelter

  • Emergency blanket or bivy
  • Lightweight tarp + cordage
  • Extra layer, hat, gloves appropriate to climate

Tools

  • Fixed-blade knife or multitool
  • Two independent fire-starting methods
  • Headlamp/flashlight + spare batteries
  • Whistle and signal mirror
  • Paper map of the area + compass

Medical & personal

  • First aid kit, personal medication (3–7 day supply)
  • Copies of ID and important documents, waterproofed
  • Cash in small denominations
  • Power bank, charging cables

Review and refresh the bag every 6 months — check batteries, food expiry, and that it still fits your current situation (kids, pets, medications).

Entry 13

Mindset

STOP

S — Stop. Don't act on panic. Sit down if you need to.
T — Think. What do you actually know? What do you need most right now?
O — Observe. Surroundings, resources, injuries, weather, time of day.
P — Plan. Pick the single next right action — then do it.

What actually predicts survival

  • Short-term, concrete goals ("gather ten branches," "reach that ridge") beat vague hope — they keep you moving and give you a sense of progress.
  • Boredom, discomfort, and fear are normal and survivable — expect them rather than treating them as signs something's wrong.
  • Positive self-talk and a sense of humor are repeatedly reported by survivors as real assets, not just nice-to-haves.
  • Rest before you're exhausted, eat before you're starving, drink before you're parched — stay ahead of your needs rather than reacting to them.

Entry 15

Urban Shelter — Abandoned Buildings

A derelict building can be faster and warmer shelter than anything you can build from scratch — but it comes with different hazards than the wilderness: structural failure, contamination, and other people. Treat it as an emergency option, not a base camp.

Step by step — before you go in
  1. Walk the outside first — look for leaning walls, sagging rooflines, or a caved-in roof. If you see these, don't enter.
  2. Test door frames and floors near the entrance with your weight before committing further in.
  3. Pick a room with a second way out, away from the most damaged-looking section of the building.
  4. Note your exit route before it gets dark or you lose light.

Structural hazards

  • Sagging floors, missing floorboards, and rotten stairs are the biggest real risk — worse than most weather.
  • Test each step or floor section before putting full weight on it; keep to the edges of a room near load-bearing walls, not the center of a span.
  • Avoid the top floor of a building that shows fire or water damage — that's usually the weakest structure.

Health hazards

  • Old insulation, ceiling tiles, and pipe wrapping may contain asbestos — avoid disturbing or breaking these materials, and don't settle in a room full of loose debris dust.
  • Rodent droppings can carry hantavirus — ventilate a room before staying, and never sweep or vacuum dry droppings; dampen the area first and avoid direct contact.
  • Standing water and mold — avoid closed-up damp rooms if you have another option.

Choosing where to shelter

  • Interior rooms without windows are quieter, more concealed, and better insulated than perimeter rooms.
  • Ground floor is easier to exit fast; upper floors are more secluded but riskier if the stairs fail — weigh that against how urgent evacuation might be.
  • Never assume wiring or gas lines are safe — treat all utilities in an abandoned building as live and hazardous unless you know otherwise.

Staying unseen and secure

  • Cover windows from the inside so light doesn't show at night.
  • Keep noise down — an abandoned building may already be in use by others (people or animals); announce yourself calmly if a space feels occupied.
  • Block or barricade the door you're not using as your exit, but never fully trap yourself in a room with only one way out.
Warning

Never light a fire inside an unventilated interior room — the same carbon monoxide risk as a tent or snow cave, worse in a sealed structure. If you need heat, crack a window for airflow first.
